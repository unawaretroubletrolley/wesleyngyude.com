The Future of Capital Markets

ETFs, AI-driven credit, tokenisation and ESG are reshaping how capital is raised and allocated, making markets more accessible and efficient, but also more concentrated and potentially fragile.

by Wesley Ng & Isaac Tan·2025·27 min read

Contents

  1. The ETF revolution and the death of mutual funds
  2. The technological revolution within debt markets
  3. ESG considerations

Introduction

The capital markets are on the cusp of their most profound transformation since the advent of electronic trading. A mix of technological innovation, changing investor preferences, and evolving social priorities is fundamentally reshaping how capital is allocated, priced, and accessed in the global economy. This essay examines the future of equity and debt capital markets specifically, and will set to explore how:

  1. The explosive growth of exchange-traded funds threatens to render mutual funds irrelevant and what this means for the future of equity markets.
  2. The evaluation of private credit’s evolution into tokenisation and AI-driven evaluations
  3. The growing emphasis on ESG considerations, signalling a broader reimagining of who participates in capital markets and what drive investment decisions.

Together, these developments suggest a future where capital markets become more accessible, transparent, and efficient, yet paradoxically more concentrated and potentially fragile—a dynamic that will define the architecture of global finance for decades to come.

Part IThe ETF revolution and the death of mutual funds

“We’re halfway there in the ETF revolution. Everything is going to be ETF’d!”Larry Fink, CEO, BlackRock

Twenty trillion dollars. That's how much capital now flows through exchange-traded funds, a financial instrument that didn't even exist before 1993. To understand the boom of ETFs, we first must dissect what an ETF is.

An ETF, or exchange-traded fund, is a basket of assets like stocks, bonds, or commodities that trades on a stock exchange like a single stock.

The key characteristics of ETF’s include:

  • Diversification — You own many different investments (stocks, bonds, etc.) in one purchase, so your risk is spread out rather than putting all your money in one company.
  • Easy to trade — You can buy and sell ETFs throughout the trading day whenever the stock market is open, just like trading regular stocks.
  • Low cost — ETFs have an average expense ratio of 0.15% as they are often passively managed
  • Accessibility — ETFs can be purchased for as little as $1 via fractional shares

If you’re familiar with the equity market, this might sound familiar to you. And you’re right, this sounds like a mutual fund.

But ETFs trump mutual funds in many facets. For starters, ETFs charge average expense ratios of just 0.15% compared to 1.01% for mutual funds, a difference that compounds significantly over time for long-term investors. Beyond cost, ETFs are structured to incur fewer capital gains taxes through their in-kind redemption process, allowing investors to defer tax liabilities that mutual fund holders cannot avoid when the fund manager rebalances the portfolio.

ETFs also offer intraday liquidity, trading throughout the day with real-time pricing, while mutual funds are priced only once at market close. This is an advantage for investors seeking to respond quickly to market movements.

Transparency further distinguishes the two: ETFs disclose their holdings daily, providing investors with complete visibility into what they own, whereas most mutual funds report quarterly, leaving investors in the dark for months at a time.

Finally, ETFs have democratised access by eliminating the prohibitive investment minimums that mutual funds typically impose. Investors can purchase ETF shares for as little as $1 through fractional shares, compared to the $2,500-$10,000 minimums common among mutual funds. Collectively, these advantages explain why capital is flooding out of mutual funds and into ETFs at an accelerating pace.

ETFs are rapidly becoming the default choice for investors ranging from retail traders to sovereign wealth funds. This inflow has also driven an impressive 12.6% CAGR for the ETF market through 2028.

More remarkably, they're expanding beyond traditional stocks and bonds to provide access to once-exclusive asset classes like collateralised loan obligations and private credit, transforming not just how people invest, but what they can invest in.

The writing is on the wall: the ETF revolution signals the beginning of the end for traditional mutual funds. But surely actively managed funds—with their sophisticated stock-picking and research capabilities—won't falter against passive index trackers?

Burton Malkiel, author of A Random Walk down Wall Street, wrote an op-ed in the Financial Times, entitled “Passive investing wins again”. The hook was an S&P report highlighting that 2024 was a bad year for active managers with 65% of all active large-cap US equity funds underperforming the S&P500.

The evidence demonstrates a compelling case for passive investment strategies. With 65% of large-cap domestic equity mutual funds underperforming the S&P 500 in 2024, the data suggests that even highly skilled fund managers struggle to consistently achieve alpha (returns above market benchmarks) after accounting for fees and transaction costs. This persistent underperformance at attempting to outperform broad market indices through active security selection has proven to be a disadvantageous endeavour.

Given that the probability of an active manager consistently outperforming a market index diminishes significantly when accounting for all associated costs, passive investment strategies offer a superior return profile with substantially lower operational complexity. This suggests that for most investors, indexing represents a more optimal allocation of capital than relying on active management to generate excess returns.

This underperformance trend has resulted in capital to increasingly flow out of traditional mutual funds and into ETFs. This shift is further validated by industry stakeholders themselves. According to a State Street survey, 25% of asset managers plan to convert at least one mutual fund into an actively managed ETF, signalling a fundamental recognition within the industry that the mutual fund structure is becoming obsolete.

What does the ETF revolution mean for the capital markets then?

The structural shift toward ETFs will implicate capital markets most consequentially in these 2 ways:

1. Price-Insensitive Flows & Index Inclusion Effects

The structural shift toward passive investing fundamentally compromises the market's price discovery mechanism by reducing the number of market participants engaged in active fundamental analysis.

This is particularly evident in index inclusion effects, where stocks experience massive price movements based purely on mechanical passive flows rather than changes in company fundamentals.

A great example of this is Tesla's approximately $60 billion valuation increase upon its S&P 500 inclusion. This gain occurred without any new earnings announcements, product innovations, or material corporate developments, driven purely by index flows.

The consequence of this is capital misallocation, where investment flows are determined by passive indexing criteria rather than economic merit, innovation. Productive enterprises receive suboptimal funding, poorly performing companies remain overvalued simply because they meet index criteria, and the stock prices loses its capacity to signal true economic value.

So, what does this mean for the individual investor? It means that:

  • It's harder to invest wisely. When prices don't reflect what companies are actually worth, it becomes difficult for individual investors to identify good deals or avoid overpriced stocks using traditional analysis.
  • They cannot rely on "the market is always right." Many people assume stock prices reflect true value. If they're increasingly driven by passive flows instead, that assumption breaks down.
  • Risk is less predictable. Investors might own a fundamentally sound company that gets crushed by index outflows or own a weak company that soars due to index inclusion. Neither reflect actual business performance.

2. Increased Correlation & Reduced Diversification

Too many ETFs create a dangerous risk because they move together in predictable ways.

As passive flows simultaneously drive entire portfolios of securities in the same direction, the principle of diversification, traditionally the cornerstone of portfolio risk management, breaks down precisely when it is most needed. During market stress events, investors discover that correlations converge toward one, eliminating the diversification benefits they anticipated. This correlation concentration creates a cascade problem: synchronised redemptions from ETFs trigger correlated selling across security baskets, accelerating downward price spirals.

This structural fragility represents the most acute tail risk from ETF growth, as the next major market correction could expose how the correlation problem transforms manageable market corrections into potentially catastrophic sell offs.

To mitigate the risks above, the future trajectory of the ETF market is likely to include a meaningful expansion of actively managed ETFs. This perspective is supported by emerging opportunities for alpha generation (producing returns beyond a passive investment benchmark) across international markets. The chart illustrates the US opportunity set for alpha capture, revealing a lower degree of correlation among securities outside the largest-cap segment, which theoretically affords active managers enhanced capacity to outperform the broad US index through superior security selection.

The decline in stock correlation can be explained by the market becoming increasingly macro-driven. In a macro-driven environment, different stocks respond differently to the same economic factors, lowering overall correlation.

For example, when the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, banks benefit from higher lending margins and their stock prices rise, while growth tech stocks suffer as higher rates reduce the present value of future earnings and their prices fall. Energy stocks remain relatively neutral, and utilities face headwinds as their dividend-heavy model becomes less attractive compared to bonds. Because all these stocks are responding to the same macro event but moving in opposite directions based on their different sensitivities to interest rates, correlation decreases.

This contrasts with a fundamentals-driven market, where individual company performance would likely push most stocks in the same direction during favourable economic conditions, creating higher correlation.

This dynamic is not confined to domestic markets; similar opportunities exist in international markets, particularly Europe, where the alpha opportunity spread remains substantial. George Michelakis, a distinguished long-short manager with a proven track record, identifies Europe as presenting a compelling landscape for active management strategies.

BlackRock classifies active ETFs into three style buckets:

  1. Alpha targeting (80% AUM/75% inflows) – fundamental and systemic strategies which seek to beat a benchmark using particular insights.
  2. Outcome-based (17% AUM/22% inflows) – income, protection and growth strategies using derivatives to target specific investment objectives.
  3. Exposure-based (3% AUM and inflows) – covering non-index strategies, like commodities or leveraged and inverse products.

The dominance of alpha-targeting active ETFs, representing 80% of active ETF assets under management, reflects the current market reality. Investors are already recognising that macro-driven markets with low correlation create genuine opportunities for active management to generate excess returns.

Active ETFs represent a structural evolution that addresses the fundamental weaknesses of traditional active mutual funds whilst inheriting the ETF advantages. The fee advantage of active

ETFs makes it materially easier for active managers to generate alpha after fees. Where traditional active mutual funds struggled to overcome their fee drag and consistently beat benchmarks, active ETFs operate with a structural cost advantage that materially improves their probability of outperformance.

Moreover, as passive index ETFs accumulate larger asset bases, they become increasingly sluggish in their rebalancing and reconstitution, creating opportunities for active ETFs to exploit mispricings and inefficiencies. This is observed through a comparison of their performances during a market downturn.

With all these advantages, it wouldn’t be a surprise to see the ETF market consisting of more actively managed funds in the near future.

Conclusion

While ETFs have demonstrated significant advantages in efficiency, transparency, and accessibility, their true resilience will ultimately be tested during periods of widespread market pessimism. Should such a downturn occur, it remains to be seen whether ETFs will maintain their stability or whether their structure would amplify market stress, contributing to economic challenges.

The direction that the ETF market would take in the years to come is speculative, and mutual funds will face their reckoning. What will define the coming decade is not merely the continued presence of ETFs, but rather how the market will adapt, either through the further expansion of actively managed ETFs or by revealing vulnerabilities that may exist beneath the surface during times of market stress. Ultimately, the way in which the ETF ecosystem responds to these challenges will shape the structure and function of equity capital markets for years to come.

But one thing is for certain: The ETF revolution has begun.

Part IIThe technological revolution within debt markets

The debt capital markets went through a fundamental transformation over the past two years with the advent of blockchain and AI-powered platforms. The convergence of such technologies — AI, tokenisation, and mobile-centric platforms go beyond mere digitalisation of our current financial processes into a more fundamental restructuring of how credit is assessed, given, and traded on an international scale.

Traditional banks now originate just 36% of LBO loans, down from 51% in 2022. Meanwhile, tokenised US Treasuries surged 260% to US$8.57 billion in a single year. However, this is not without its challenges: regulatory frameworks remain fragmented, and secondary markets are nascent—in two decades of fintech innovation, only 3% of traditional banking revenues have been disrupted. In this report we will examine real implementations, and the gap between potential technologies and the reality of markets today.

AI-Powered Platforms

AI and machine learning achieved dramatic improvements in credit assessments, moving from experimental to real market-deployment. Upstart, a leading AI-driven lending platform, exemplifies this shift by fully automating 92% of its loans through advanced neural networks. These models analyse over 2,500 variables per application, significantly surpassing the traditional credit risk scorecards, which typically consider only 15 to 20 variables.

Upstart’s system is also designed for continuous improvement, processing 85,839+ daily repayment events to continuously retrain models. This approach has led Upstart to achieve 17 percentage points better separation accuracy than conventional credit models, translating to 73% more borrowers approved at the same default rate, or alternatively, reducing defaults by 53% while maintaining the same approval rate.

This resilience was particularly evident during COVID 19 payment shock. Upstart powered loans displayed a 40% lower impairment increase compared to industry’s average while their AI risk tier system proved 6-times more effective than credit score bands at predicting payment behaviours and outcomes.

The integration of AI has also brought about Operational cost reductions across the industry. Financial institutions implementing AI experience 20-70% operational cost reductions. For example, Freddie Mac’s 2024 study highlighted the impact of AI on retail loan processing, with AI being able to reduce retail loan processing costs by 40%, from an average of US$11,600 to US$7,000 per loan.

In the realm of credit underwriting, H2O.ai experienced significant annual savings too, reporting US$20 million annual savings in credit underwriting for a single customer. They achieved this by deploying gradient boosted machines and neural networks that have the ability to render decisions in milliseconds rather than days.

Customer service operations have also experienced a reduction in costs. Klarna, for example, achieved US$40 million in annual customer savings by deploying AI chatbots. These chatbots handle 2.3 million monthly conversations (equivalent to 700 full time employees), while reducing response times from 11 minutes to under 2 minutes.

AI has also led to unprecedented advancements in processing speed and accuracy. For instance, Klarna processes over 100 data points per transaction through machine learning algorithms, delivering instant automated approval decisions, and real time risk-assessment for each purchase attempt. This real-time risk assessment for every purchase attempt not only streamlines the approval process but also enhances the accuracy of credit decisions.

Similarly, Upstart’s Credit’s Decision API is able to deliver approvals in under three seconds, with 92% of cases requiring zero human intervention. This improves processing speed by 25x compared to traditional credit processes that take anywhere from 1-4 weeks.

Not only are transactions faster, but they are more accurate too. By evaluating each transaction individually rather than issuing static credit lines, the transaction-level underwriting enables more accurate risks assessment based on current financial state and purchase context.

AI-powered lending platforms have demonstrated their effectiveness and scalability not only in consumer lending, but also through tangible financial performance and risk management outcomes too. Affirm, for example, has expanded its reach to 23 million active users, achieving a notable 24% year-over-year growth. This rapid expansion is matched by a significant financial turnaround, as Affirm reported a net income of US$69.2 million, reversing a US$45.1 million loss from the previous year.

Risk metrics further highlight the advantages of AI-driven underwriting. Affirm maintains delinquency rates that are three to four times lower than those observed with traditional credit cards, all while serving a consumer base that is frequently excluded from prime credit markets. Similarly, Klarna has achieved a global credit loss rate of just 0.39%, representing a 41% reduction year over year. The implementation of AI-powered underwriting processes has enabled Klarna to reduce credit losses by 56%, while consistently keeping default rates below 1%.

Despite the notable benefits and advancements brought by AI in the lending sector, several significant challenges continue to temper enthusiasm for widespread adoption.

According to IBM surveys, only 16% of AI projects successfully scale across enterprises, highlighting the difficulty of implementing these technologies at scale. Furthermore, just 24% of AI initiatives deliver their initially promised ROI, with many projects falling short of expected outcomes.

Additionally, implementation costs represent an additional substantial barrier for financial institutions seeking to deploy AI solutions. Deloitte’s 2022 survey found 40% of lenders identified implementation cost as a major barrier limiting AI uptake. Basic AI credit systems costing an average of US$250,000 while advanced neural networks with multiple data sources costing an average of US$650,000 according to Science Soft estimates.

This considerable investment may deter some organisations from embracing AI, particularly those with limited resources or uncertain projected returns.

Institutionalising Tokenisation

The tokenised real-world asset sector has reached a significant milestone, with 2.1 billion in assets now tokenised—the largest amount ever recorded for this category. This achievement highlights the growing acceptance and integration of tokenised assets in financial markets.

Tokenised assets have the potential to bring about tremendous efficiency and automation enhancements to traditional debt markets. For instance, a major breakthrough was demonstrated in August 2025, when JPMorgan’s Kinexys platform (previously known as Onyx) successfully processed a US$100 million commercial paper transaction in collaboration with OCBC and State Street. The transaction showcased fully automated T+0 settlement, meaning that the settlement was completed on the same day without any manual intervention.

State Street, a global leader with US$49 trillion in assets under custody, has further advanced the integration of blockchain technology in financial services. The institution launched blockchain custody services for tokenised debt securities on the Kinexys platform, marking a significant industry development. With this move, State Street became the first third-party custodian to offer dedicated blockchain custody for tokenised debt securities, setting a new standard for asset security and institutional trust in the tokenised asset space.

Concurrently, the tokenised US Treasury market exploded, registering a 260% increase from US$769 million in January 2024 to US$8.57 billion by October 2025. Similarly, the broader tokenised real-world asset market reached US$17.88 billion in March 2025, up from US$10 billion the year prior. Private credit is dominant in this market, comprising 61% of total value. US Treasuries account for 30%, commodities represent 7% and institutional funds make up the remaining 2%.

The number of Token holders also grew substantially to 54,572 across 52 products. These products offer a competitive 3.82% seven-day annual percentage yield (APY), appealing to investors seeking both security and attractive returns. Ethereum continues to dominate this market, accounting for US$1.46 billion in tokenised assets. However, the ecosystem is rapidly diversifying, with expansion into networks such as Stellar, Solana, Mantle, and Base.

This dramatic expansion of tokenised assets underscores the accelerating adoption and recognition of tokenised securities within the broader financial ecosystem.

While the progress of tokenisation in the financial sector is clear, the gap between its potential and current reality remains substantial.

One of the main obstacles is limited secondary market liquidity constrains for tokenised debt trading — most investors hold positions long-term rather than actively trading. This behaviour undermines the anticipated liquidity premium that tokenisation was expected to deliver.

Additionally, corporate treasuries tend to park tokens on balance sheets but seldom use them as collateral for further financing. This limited utilisation further challenges the narrative that tokenisation would unlock greater liquidity in the market.

Additionally, standardisation deficits plague the industry: no industry-wide standards exist for smart contract formats across platforms, creating compatibility issues and fragmenting liquidity across incompatible “walled gardens”.

Integration is further complicated by the use of varying computer languages and data models across projects. As a result, many initiatives end up creating what are essentially “dumb tokens travelling on new rails”—simply digital representations of existing assets that travel on new technological rails, but do not provide machine-executable, deterministic definitions of cash flow.

These challenges highlight the critical areas that must be addressed for tokenised assets to achieve their full promise of enhanced liquidity, interoperability, and automation in capital markets.

Moreover, the advancement of tokenised assets and decentralised finance (DeFi) platforms brings with it significant technical vulnerabilities which require ongoing vigilance. One of the largest vulnerabilities lie with smart contracts, which remain susceptible to hacking and coding flaws.

DeFi platforms have already fallen victim to numerous exploits, totalling hundreds of millions in losses. These incidents underscore the inherent risks associated with deploying smart contracts, which, once deployed, are difficult to modify or upgrade without complex governance mechanisms. Further complications arise from the “oracle problem”, where smart contracts are inherently unable to directly access real-world data directly, creating dependencies on services like Chainlink for essential price feeds and real-world asset (RWA) valuations. These services, while pivotal for the functioning of smart contracts, also introduces opportunities for potential manipulation or failure vectors. Innovative attacks such as flash loan attacks and miner extractable value exploitation have also emerged, presenting novel risks absent in traditional finance.

Thus, it is imperative that these vulnerabilities are addressed as tokenisation and DeFi continue its rapid expansion into global financial markets.

Institutional actors are responding to these challenges by investing heavily in blockchain security infrastructure. For instance, BNY Mellon has invested approximately US$50 million to the development of a multi-signature blockchain technology security infrastructure, enhancing security and custody purposes.

These investments demonstrate the capital requirements for levels of institutional grade custody that will be necessary for widespread adoption and trust in blockchain based services.

Conclusion

The transformation of debt capital markets and private credit in 2024-2025 represents not just the digitisation of existing processes but fundamental restructuring of how credit is assessed, distributed and traded. The market is witnessing three parallel evolutions, private credit displacing bank lending, AI revolutionising underwriting, and blockchain enabling tokenisation.

The emerging structure of the debt capital markets reflects a shift towards ecosystem collaboration more than a winner-take-all disruption.

In this new paradigm, traditional and emerging players contribute their respective strengths to build a more robust and efficient market. Banks continue to leverage their client relationships to originate loans, drawing on their established networks and trust within the financial system.

Private credit funds provide permanent capital without the balance sheet constraints that typically affect banks, and fintech firms supply advanced technology and scale customer acquisition. Meanwhile, tokenisation platforms offer infrastructure, facilitating the digital representation and transfer of assets. Each participant contributes their unique comparative advantage, resulting in a collaborative ecosystem.

Recent examples illustrate how technology can serve to empower and not displace incumbent institutions too, with First Hawaiian Bank, through its partnership with Zest AI, saw a 13x increase in automated decision making. This partnership demonstrates the potential for technology to enhance traditional practices rather than render them obsolete.

Similarly, JPMorgan's $100 million tokenised commercial paper issuance on its own platform demonstrates how incumbents are leading innovation and actively shaping the trajectory of marketing transformation, rather than lagging behind.

The transformation of debt capital markets remains both unfinished and uncertain. Technology has played a pivotal role in enabling change, but it has not assured the dominance of private credit. In fact, the rise of private credit owes much not only to technological advancements but also to regulatory pressures on bank capital requirements and the ongoing demand from investors for higher yields. These external forces have been just as influential as technology in shaping the market landscape.

Artificial intelligence has contributed to improvements in lending processes, but its impact has fallen short of a true revolution. For example, only 24% of AI-driven projects have delivered tangible returns on investment, and cases such as Klarna’s retreat from excessive automation highlight the execution risks and limitations of relying solely on technology.

Similarly, tokenisation has demonstrated that it is possible to digitise and fractionalise debt instruments; however, it has not proven to be essential for all market segments. Traditional debt instruments continue to function effectively for most use cases, and the strongest value proposition for tokenisation lies in assets that were previously illiquid or difficult to access in fractional form.

While technology has made strides in expanding financial inclusion, substantial barriers remain. Despite dramatic progress, approximately 300 million people across Southeast Asia— and comparable populations in other regions—still face exclusion due to inadequate infrastructure, limited financial literacy, or restrictive regulatory environments. These challenges underscore that the market’s transformation is ongoing and that significant work remains to ensure the benefits of innovation are broadly realised.

Over the past two years, it has become clear that technology can fundamentally restructure debt capital markets. Private credit has demonstrated the potential to scale into the trillions, highlighting its capacity to serve a vast array of borrowers. Artificial intelligence now enables the instantaneous assessment of millions of borrowers, streamlining the underwriting process and driving significant efficiencies. At the same time, blockchain technology has made it possible to settle transactions worth hundreds of millions of dollars within a single day, accelerating the pace of market operations.

As the sector moves forward, the critical question is whether these advancements will translate into enduring competitive advantages or if they will remain as temporary, technology-driven disruptions that are eventually integrated and absorbed by established institutions. The outcome is likely to differ across various segments of the market. For example, private credit is structurally positioned to excel in middle market lending, where its flexibility and scale offer distinct benefits. Artificial intelligence, regardless of the institution deploying it, promises to deliver lasting cost reductions by automating processes at scale. Meanwhile, tokenisation is most valuable in market segments where enhanced liquidity or accessibility address previously unfulfilled needs.

While the transformation of debt capital markets is both tangible and measurable, its ultimate trajectory will depend on evolving factors such as credit cycles, regulatory developments, and shifting competitive dynamics. The market’s future shape remains to be fully determined as these elements continue to unfold.

Part IIIESG considerations

No Longer just an afterthought

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) considerations have evolved from peripheral concerns to central drivers reshaping global capital markets. What began as a checkbox requirement has now become a fundamental force, influencing how companies access capital, how investors allocate resources, and how markets price risk and opportunity. This transformation is not merely cosmetic, but a shift in the foundational principles underlying both debt and equity capital markets, challenging decades of established practice built primarily around credit risk and financial returns.

Why has ESG been on the rise?

There is no doubt that ESG investing has been on the rise, with the market for ESG investing projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of approximately 18.82% from 2025 to 2034, a near 5x increase over the period.

ESG investing has surged in recent years due to several factors:

  1. Regulatory pressure is intensifying globally, with more than 80 countries adopting ESG reporting laws and over 2000 regulations now making transparency mandatory.
  2. Climate urgency has made environmental risk impossible to ignore, with 84% of S&P 500 companies now reporting climate change as a material risk factor. This is an increase from just 67% of companies back in 2021.
  3. There has been a generational shift, with more young investors flooding the market, a stronger emphasis has now been placed on sustainability and social responsibility. Additionally, young investors are increasingly demanding that companies align with their values too.

These factors have made ESG a focal point of many companies, and corporate leaders are starting to recognise it as a business imperative. 82% of US CEOs now identify ESG as a key strategic value driver, understanding that integrating ESG into their company’s core mission is inevitable.

Today, ESG is no longer seen as a mere requirement to fulfil but as a competitive advantage for future success.

How Will This Affect Equity and Debt Capital Markets?

Slaughter and May's briefing to the COP26 conference articulates “the most important question facing capital markets is whether the structures, documentation, practice, and regulation that have governed debt and equity issuance for decades need fundamental amendment to adapt to the shift in emphasis towards ESG.”

Traditionally, debt investors have focused exclusively on creditworthiness—the ability of issuers to pay interest and repay principal. Equity investors, while considering broader strategic factors, have primarily evaluated companies through the lens of financial returns.

However, investors are now investing for reasons other than pure economic returns—to achieve climate change mitigation, social impact, or governance improvements—this represents what Slaughter and May calls "a revolutionary change."

The result is that practices within the two capital markets are starting to reflect these changed investor priorities and broader societal objectives.

The Geography of Conviction

The global ESG landscape has fractured along predictable fault lines. While Europe continues to lead the charge, the US market faces increasing fragmentation due to political divisions and legal challenges, while Asia-Pacific is emerging as a powerhouse.

In the US, the backlash has been swift and coordinated. Major banks including Bank of America, Citigroup, Morgan Stanley, JP Morgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, and Wells Fargo left the United Nations backed Net-Zero Banking Alliance between late 2024 and early 2025. BlackRock CEO Larry Fink said he would stop mentioning ESG altogether after it had become politically “weaponised”, with his firm losing business from some state entities. Texas alone terminated a US$8.5 billion investment managed by BlackRock, citing a state law that prohibits investing in firms seen as hostile to the fossil fuel industry.

Yet even as American institutions retreat from climate alliances, the fundamentals have yet to change. A study by Robeco found that while policy changes since Trump’s election have impacted investment decisions, the importance of climate issues to investors has not declined, with nearly two-thirds expecting climate change to become central or significant to their investment policies in the next two years. More tellingly, the new administration’s anti-climate policies are driving investors to look for climate-related investment opportunities outside the US.

Meanwhile Europe faces its own reckoning, but from a regulatory fatigue rather than ideological opposition. The European Commission is revisiting the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive framework to reduce compliance burdens, potentially leading to a significant cut in the scope of companies subject to the regulation and standards simplification. Companies are balking at the sheer volume of data points they are being asked to provide, on everything from the gender diversity of their boards to the biodiversity risks posed by their operations.

Asia-Pacific countries including Hong Kong, Singapore, Korea, and Japan are advancing ESG disclosure requirements alongside corporate reforms, taking a more pragmatic approach that ensures regulations align with market dynamics. China’s trajectory is particularly significant, issuing voluntary ESG reporting standards in late 2024 that lay the groundwork for a more comprehensive national ESG framework.

Equity Capital Markets

The proliferation of ESG is creating tangible economic effects in the equity capital markets. Here are some of the most obvious impacts:

Valuation Effects: Companies with strong ESG profiles increasingly command valuation premiums as institutional mandates and retail investor preferences direct capital toward sustainable investments. This "ESG premium" manifests in higher price-to-earnings ratios, lower volatility, and improved analyst coverage. Conversely, companies in carbon-intensive industries or those with poor ESG practices face persistent discounts, which is known as an "ESG penalty", that directly impacts their cost of equity capital.

Capital Access Constraints: For certain industries, particularly fossil fuels and carbon-intensive sectors, the pool of available equity investors is shrinking dramatically. Major institutional investors are implementing hard exclusions and removing entire sectors from their portfolios. A recent survey found that 85% of institutional investors now integrate sustainability- related criteria into their investment decisions. For companies that do not prioritise ESG, it means traditional public equity financing is becoming increasingly unavailable, forcing companies to rely on alternative forms of capital.

Index Construction and Passive Investing: The growth of passive investing has made index inclusion pivotal for capital access, and as ESG-screened indices are rapidly becoming mainstream, companies failing to meet ESG criteria face exclusion from the enormous pool of passive capital now dominating equity markets. Index providers have become gatekeepers whose ESG screening methodologies determine which companies can access these massive capital flows. Companies excluded from major ESG indices like the S&P 500 ESG or MSCI ESG Leaders will lose out on a huge chunk of capital from passive investments.

Greener IPOs: Companies planning to go public increasingly face heightened scrutiny of their ESG credentials. Prospectus requirements have evolved from traditionally focusing solely on financial risks and creditworthiness to demanding more comprehensive ESG disclosure.

Companies must now address climate change financial risks, articulate ESG strategies and targets, demonstrate board diversity and independence, ensure supply chain sustainability, and quantify social impact across stakeholder groups.

Institutional investors are increasingly making ESG performance a prerequisite for IPO participation, creating pressure on issuers to demonstrate not just historical ESG performance, but credible forward-looking commitments and transition plans. According to the EY 2024 Institutional Investor Survey of 350 decision-makers, 88% say their institution has substantially increased its use of ESG information over the past year. Companies that fail to meet these expectations risk limiting their investor base, reducing execution certainty, and leaving valuation on the table.

Debt Capital Markets

While public markets wrestle with politicised terminology, private credit has emerged as perhaps the most significant development in sustainable finance. The private credit market grew to nearly US$2 trillion in assets under management in 2024, expanding far beyond its direct lending roots into asset-backed finance and bespoke structured tags. Unlike their public market counterparts, private credit players face less political scrutiny while maintaining deeper engagement with portfolio companies.

In an FTI Consulting survey of more than 500 global private equity leaders, 64% reported seeing ESG as a viable value lever, either significantly or situationally. This is value creation —private markets data from over 9000 portfolio companies shows that firms have found that driving decarbonisation and improving the employee experience can reduce costs and strengthen revenues.

The reason private credit can pursue ESG integration more aggressively lies in its structure. Private capital managers can be powerful agents of change as they are more likely to have hands-on approach within areas such as real estate and infrastructure, where they can have a real impact on issues such as reducing carbon emissions or increasing biodiversity. With longer investment horizons, concentrated ownership, and direct board representation, private credit managers can embed sustainability criteria in loan covenants and monitor progress without the performative quarterly earnings dance of public markets.

KKR’s “ESG Credit 2.0” framework exemplifies this evolution —scoring investment opportunities against proprietary materiality-based ESG scorecards, analysing whether companies provide sustainable solutions, and embedding ESG-related information rights in finance documents. It is a far cry from the checkbox compliance that characterised earlier ESG efforts.

The Greenwashing Reckoning

The maturation of ESG debt markets has brought uncomfortable questions about authenticity to the forefront. Sustainability-linked loans have come under increasing scrutiny in 2025, with critics highlighting their lack of enforceable environmental standards, transparency, and measurable targets.

The cases are piling up. Deutsche Bank’s asset management arm, DWS, was fined €25 million by German prosecutors for misleading investors about its ESG credentials. More dramatically, companies like Shell and Drax were revealed to have secured sustainability-linked loans while continuing high pollution activities, with loan terms linked to vague or non-binding sustainability metrics. In April 2025, the Royal Bank of Canada publicly abandoned its sustainable finance goals, citing the absence of clear definitions and accountability mechanisms.

The UK’s Competition and Markets Authority announced it will begin large-scale public enforcement of its Green Claims Code from Autumn 2025, signalling that regulators are moving from guidance to consequences. The days of aspirational, unverifiable sustainability gains are ending, replaced by hard metrics and legal liability.

The Trillion Dollar Question - What Comes Next?

Despite the turbulence, the ESG investment market is projected to reach US$40 trillion by 2030—a projection that suggests growth will remain strong despite political and regulatory challenges. But the nature of that growth will look different from its explosion of the early 2020s.

Several forces will shape the next chapter of ESG:

Standardisation and simplification: IOSCO issued a statement of support in November 2024 and encouraged the use of ISSA 5000 as the new global reference point for assurance across its 130 jurisdictions. After decades of fragmentation, global sustainability reporting standards are finally coalescing —not through moral persuasion but through the brute force of institutional infrastructure.

Tech as an enabler: As AI becomes integral to sustainable finance, companies like Refinitiv are developing AI tools that ensure transparency and fairness in ESG data analysis. Real-time monitoring of supply chains, automated sustainability audits and blockchain verified carbon credits will transform ESG from a compliance exercise into an operational reality.

Private markets ascendancy: Private companies and private markets will play a growing role in shaping ESG financing, as new reporting regulations increasingly apply to privately held firms and a broad range of private funders embed ESG factors in their investment decisions. With 87% of private market firms now having personnel dedicated to managing day to day ESG affairs, the infrastructure for sustained integration is in place.

ESG integration into capital markets is inevitable. The central question has shifted from whether to integrate ESG principles to determining the methods and mechanisms by which this integration will occur.

Key areas of focus would include: How will regulations evolve? What will be the standardised reporting standard that will emerge? Which procedures will become the industry standard?

The answers to these critical questions will shape the direction and character of capital markets in the coming years, setting the foundation for the next era of responsible and sustainable finance.
