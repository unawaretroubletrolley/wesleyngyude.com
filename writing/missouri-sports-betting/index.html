Can Missouri bank on sports betting?

Nearly a year into legalization, billions have been wagered but the tax take is modest. The culprit isn't low demand or bettors fleeing casinos. It's how the tax was designed.

by Wesley Ng Yu De·2026·4 min read

In December 2025, Missouri legalized sports betting, joining the growing number of states seeking to capitalize on the burgeoning, multi-billion dollar industry. On paper, the proposal seemed enticing. Winning for Missouri Education, the campaign organization spearheading the legalization effort, touted the prospect of substantial new tax revenue, projecting millions in additional funding for Missouri's public schools. However, nearly a year into legalization, the early numbers are dubious. Despite billions of dollars in wagers, tax collections have remained relatively modest, and the question has been raised: to what extent can the state of Missouri rely on sports betting as a meaningful source of tax revenue?

Missouri voters approved Amendment 2 in November 2024, allowing legal sports betting to begin in December 2025. Under the law, sportsbooks pay a 10% tax on adjusted gross revenue, rather than the total amount wagered. Operators can deduct winnings paid to bettors and certain promotional expenses, reducing how much revenue is taxable. After regulatory and problem-gambling costs, the remaining tax revenue is directed toward public education. Before legalization, Missouri officials projected about $28.9 million in annual tax revenue, while the Winning for Missouri Education campaign advertised $100 million over five years. However, these projections depend heavily on how much sportsbooks actually earn and how much they can deduct.

Finding 1Missouri's low take comes from tax design, not low betting

Missouri's sports betting tax currently raises little, with about $21.6 million a year at its post launch pace, or roughly 5% of what the state collects from casinos. This is not because Missourians bet little, but is largely attributed instead to how the tax is designed. Ohio, which launched with a different structure, shows what a revised design could achieve. Ohio residents bet about 1.7 times as much per person as Missourians, yet Ohio collects about 5 times as much tax per person.

Promotions matter most when a market opens, because operators use free bets and bonuses heavily to win new customers. In Missouri's first month, December 2025, operators handed out more than $125 million in promotions and the state collected just $521,000 on $543 million in bets.

Ohio took the opposite approach. It barred promotional deductions for its first four years and is phasing them in gradually, so operators' promotions did not shrink the tax base. Applying Ohio's yield of $2.03 per $100 bet to Missouri's roughly $3.26 billion in annual bets gives about $66 million a year, around three times what Missouri collects now.

Finding 2Sports betting isn't yet substituting for casino gambling, but the tax gap makes it a possible risk

Sports betting only adds revenue if it is new spending. Missouri casinos pay a 21% tax on adjusted gross revenue, versus 10% for sportsbooks, so every dollar that moves from casinos to betting costs the state at least 11 cents and the data shows little sign of that shift. In November 2025, the month before launch, casino revenue was up 3.3% year over year. Since launch, casino revenue has risen year over year in seven of the nine months, from 4.6% in December to 5.4% in July, with only March (down 1.6%) and August (down 3.5%) falling. The average post-launch monthly change is about 3.5%, in line with the pre-launch baseline. Casino-based retail sportsbooks also took only $17.9 million of the $1.53 billion wagered through March, about 1.2%, since nearly all betting happens on mobile apps.

One caution is table games, which fell 10.8% in March and 17.7% in April while slots held steady or grew. This is a pattern to monitor, but right now it is too limited to prove substitution. In conclusion, Missouri's low sports betting revenue is not explained by bettors leaving casinos. Tax design explains it far better. Substitution deserves monitoring, but fixing promotional deductions is what would make the revenue meaningful.
